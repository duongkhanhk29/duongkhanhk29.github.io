Many academic papers are difficult to read because good ideas are often hidden behind unclear writing. Reading a wall of text, a thousand citations, and twenty numerical tables can cause readers to give up. This is not necessary. Writing is not just reporting research after it is finished; it is a level of critical thinking and the final test of my understanding. These are my personal ways of article writing, shared for reference, reflecting how I think about each part from the first sentence to the last reference.

I always start my introduction with a hook to catch the reader's attention. This hook is not a clever phrase, but a clear statement of where the academic conversation currently stands. I state the current debate, the gap in that debate, my novelty, and my contribution clearly and early. My goal is to help the reader understand why the paper matters rather than making them work to figure it out. At the end of the introduction, I add a short outline of the paper. This outline acts like a promise, providing a map that shows the destination, the route, and the reasons for taking that path, avoiding a confusing introduction where the reader gets lost before the journey begins.

When writing the literature review, I avoid making a list of papers in chronological order. Stating who said what in which year is like writing a phone book; it shows only that titles and dates can be copied. Instead, I tell a story by weaving theories into one big picture to show how they connect, agree, and conflict. Constructively challenging assumptions moves knowledge forward. I position theories within the larger intellectual landscape to evaluate where they succeed, where they fail, and what they leave out. Once that empty space on the canvas is identified, I show what I add to fill it. My contribution is not a small tweak, but the missing puzzle piece that makes the whole picture make sense. Broad thinking is shown not by citing everything, but by seeing the shape of the entire conversation and stepping in where something is missing.

In the data and methods section, I avoid copying methods from other papers, which suggests a lack of understanding. I build a clear framework that operationalises my theoretical ideas and directly addresses my research question, serving as a bridge between abstract theories and concrete numbers. I explain every choice from first principles, detailing why specific data and variables were chosen, what would be lost with alternative datasets, and why that loss is acceptable. Every choice is an argument, not an accident. I ensure I understand the principles and assumptions of the method under the hood, explaining how it works in plain terms. Standard methods rarely fit non standard questions perfectly, so I modify and customize them by adding a lag, dropping a control, or adjusting a threshold. These modifications reflect active thinking rather than weakness or cheating. I write the methods section as if generating new findings, showing deliberate choices that shaped the results.

For the results and discussion, I am highly selective about what I report. I do not dump every test, robustness check, or alternative specification. Statistics exist solely to support the story. Descriptive statistics, such as means, standard deviations, and correlations, belong in the appendix because the average sample age is usually not necessary to understand the main argument. I use visualisations whenever possible, as clear graphs communicate stories quickly and stay in the memory longer. When tables are necessary, I redesign them completely to reframe the narrative: bolding key coefficients, grouping items logically rather than alphabetically, and adding interpretive notes directly in the table so the reader does not have to squint or cross reference. In the discussion, I move beyond comparing effect sizes to ask deeper questions about the drivers, underlying mechanisms, and new insights gained from the findings. The focus is on extracting meaning rather than reporting numerical differences.

My conclusion is a reflection rather than a copy of the abstract. While the abstract is a preview, the conclusion reflects on the study. I include limitations openly, covering what the study could not do, where the data fell short, and what potential errors existed in my assumptions. Limitations are honest statements about the boundaries of my knowledge, not confessions of failure. I also point to future research, telling readers where to go next if they wish to build on my work. A constructive conclusion states three things: what was learned, what could not be learned, and where to go next. This generous approach helps the field grow.

Referencing choices also reveal paper quality immediately. I maintain high quality by citing peer reviewed articles or books from reputable presses, avoiding working papers, blog posts, or unpublished manuscripts unless there is an extraordinary reason. Piling a hundred citations behind a single claim is lazy writing that hides which papers actually matter. I follow a triple standard for selective referencing: relevance, influence, and recency, usually citing no more than three references per claim to avoid noise. Instead of grouping multiple citations in one pair of parentheses, I break claims into smaller pieces. For example, rather than combining evidence from two distinct countries into one generic claim, I state the evidence for each country separately with its respective reference, respecting differences in context and demonstrating precision.

Regarding the overall paper layout, sticking to the traditional structure of introduction, literature review, data and methods, results and discussion, and conclusion is a safe and familiar choice for early career researchers. However, the layout can be tailored to fit specific research ideas. If two research questions are deeply interconnected, such that the outcome of the first is the direct motivation for the second, a custom layout may be necessary. The guiding rule is simple: help the reader follow easily. Clarity is sacred, while layout is secondary. Writing is the final test of my thinking, and if I cannot explain my work clearly, I do not truly understand it. These are my ways of writing, intended to help create papers that are read, understood, and remembered.
